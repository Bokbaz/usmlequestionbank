### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1038
Exam: Step 2 CK
System: Pregnancy
Discipline: Obstetrics & Gynecology
Competency: Pharmacotherapy
Category: Obstetric complications
Topic: Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy
Difficulty: 3
Tags: eclampsia, seizure, magnesium

Stem:
A 24-year-old primigravid woman at 36 weeks' gestation is brought to the emergency department after a generalized tonic-clonic seizure at home that lasted about 1 minute. Earlier today, she had a severe headache and blurred vision. Her blood pressure was normal at a prenatal visit 3 weeks ago. She is now drowsy but arousable and is not seizing. Her pulse is 98/min, respirations are 18/min, and blood pressure is 172/112 mm Hg. Oxygen saturation is 96% on room air. Examination shows 3+ pitting edema of the lower extremities and hyperreflexia without focal neurologic deficits. Urine dipstick shows 3+ protein. The fetal heart rate tracing shows a baseline of 150/min with moderate variability. She is placed in the left lateral decubitus position, and supplemental oxygen is administered.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. CT scan of the head
B. Emergency cesarean delivery
C. Intravenous lorazepam
D. Intravenous magnesium sulfate
E. Intravenous phenytoin

Answer: D
Key concept: Magnesium sulfate for eclampsia

Explanation:
New-onset hypertension (172/112 mm Hg, a severe range) and proteinuria after 20 weeks' gestation is **preeclampsia**; a **new generalized seizure** with no other cause makes it **eclampsia**. Headache, visual disturbance, and hyperreflexia are warning signs that preceded it.

Priorities after airway, breathing, circulation, and lateral positioning (already done):

1. **Intravenous magnesium sulfate**: a loading dose of 4 to 6 g over 15 to 20 minutes, then 1 to 2 g/h. Magnesium is superior to benzodiazepines and phenytoin for **stopping and preventing recurrent eclamptic seizures** and reduces maternal death. It likely works by cerebral vasodilation and NMDA receptor antagonism.
2. **Treat severe-range blood pressure** within 30 to 60 minutes with intravenous labetalol, intravenous hydralazine, or oral immediate-release nifedipine, to prevent stroke.
3. **Deliver once the mother is stabilized.** Eclampsia is an indication for delivery, but it does not by itself require emergency cesarean; induction is reasonable when the fetal status is reassuring, as here.

Monitor for magnesium toxicity: **loss of deep tendon reflexes first**, then respiratory depression, then cardiac arrest. Check reflexes, respiratory rate, urine output, and renal function (magnesium is renally cleared). The antidote is **calcium gluconate**.

Option explanations:
A. Head imaging is indicated for focal deficits, prolonged coma, seizures that recur despite magnesium, or atypical timing. It should not delay magnesium therapy in typical eclampsia.
B. Delivery is the definitive treatment, but the mother must be stabilized first. With a reassuring fetal tracing, there is no need for an emergency cesarean before magnesium and blood pressure control.
C. Benzodiazepines are reserved for seizures that persist despite magnesium; they are less effective at preventing recurrence and add sedation and respiratory depression.
D. Correct. Intravenous magnesium sulfate is first-line to treat and prevent eclamptic seizures.
E. Phenytoin is inferior to magnesium sulfate for eclampsia.

Objective: Eclampsia is treated with intravenous magnesium sulfate (to stop and prevent seizures) plus urgent control of severe-range blood pressure, followed by delivery once the mother is stabilized.

Textbook:
**Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy**

| Disorder | Definition |
|---|---|
| Chronic hypertension | Hypertension before pregnancy or before 20 weeks |
| Gestational hypertension | New hypertension at or after 20 weeks, no proteinuria or end-organ damage |
| Preeclampsia | New hypertension at or after 20 weeks with proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction |
| Preeclampsia with severe features | BP 160/110 or higher, platelets under 100,000, liver transaminases twice normal or RUQ pain, creatinine over 1.1, pulmonary edema, new headache or visual symptoms |
| Eclampsia | Preeclampsia plus new-onset seizures |
| HELLP syndrome | Hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets |
| Superimposed preeclampsia | Chronic hypertension that develops preeclampsia |

- **Pathogenesis:** abnormal trophoblast invasion and failed remodeling of spiral arteries; placental ischemia releases antiangiogenic factors (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1) that cause systemic endothelial dysfunction.
- **Risk factors:** nulliparity, prior preeclampsia, chronic hypertension, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, antiphospholipid syndrome, multifetal gestation, obesity, age over 35.
- **Prevention:** low-dose aspirin from 12 weeks in high-risk patients.
- **Delivery timing:** gestational hypertension or preeclampsia without severe features at 37 weeks; with severe features at 34 weeks (or earlier if unstable); eclampsia and HELLP after stabilization.
- **Antihypertensives in pregnancy:** labetalol, nifedipine, hydralazine, methyldopa. Avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and direct renin inhibitors (fetal renal toxicity).
