### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1031
Exam: Step 1
System: Immune System
Discipline: Pharmacology
Competency: Pharmacotherapy
Category: Immunologically mediated disorders
Topic: Hereditary angioedema
Difficulty: 3
Tags: bradykinin, complement, ACE inhibitors

Stem:
A 34-year-old woman comes to the office to establish care. Since adolescence, she has had recurrent episodes of nonpruritic swelling of her face, lips, and hands, each lasting 2 to 4 days; some episodes are accompanied by severe, crampy abdominal pain. The swelling is not accompanied by hives and does not improve with antihistamines. One episode began the day after a dental procedure. Her mother has had similar episodes. Her blood pressure today is 152/96 mm Hg, and it was similarly increased at two previous visits. Examination shows no abnormalities. Serum C4 concentration is decreased, and C1 esterase inhibitor concentration and function are decreased.

Lead-in: Which of the following antihypertensive medications is most likely to worsen this patient's underlying condition?

A. Amlodipine
B. Chlorthalidone
C. Hydralazine
D. Lisinopril
E. Metoprolol

Answer: D
Key concept: ACE inhibitors are contraindicated in hereditary angioedema

Explanation:
Recurrent episodes of **nonpitting, nonpruritic swelling without urticaria**, abdominal attacks (bowel wall edema), trauma-triggered attacks (dental work), an affected parent (autosomal dominant), **low C4**, and **low C1 esterase inhibitor (C1-INH)** confirm **hereditary angioedema (HAE)**.

C1-INH normally restrains two systems:
- The **classical complement pathway**: without it, C1 is continuously active and consumes **C4** (hence the low C4, the best screening test).
- The **contact (kallikrein-kinin) system**: without it, plasma kallikrein cleaves high-molecular-weight kininogen into **bradykinin**.

**Bradykinin is the mediator of swelling in HAE.** It acts on B2 receptors to increase vascular permeability. That is why HAE does not respond to antihistamines, corticosteroids, or reliably to epinephrine.

**Angiotensin-converting enzyme is the same enzyme as kininase II**, which degrades bradykinin. ACE inhibitors such as **lisinopril** therefore raise bradykinin levels (the same mechanism behind ACE-inhibitor cough and angioedema in people without HAE). In a patient with HAE, they can provoke severe, potentially fatal airway swelling and are contraindicated.

Option explanations:
A. Amlodipine, a dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker, causes dependent peripheral edema through precapillary arteriolar dilation, not bradykinin-mediated angioedema. It is a reasonable choice for this patient.
B. Chlorthalidone, a thiazide-like diuretic, causes hypokalemia, hyponatremia, and hyperuricemia but does not affect bradykinin.
C. Hydralazine can cause drug-induced lupus and reflex tachycardia, but it does not affect bradykinin degradation.
D. Correct. ACE inhibitors block bradykinin breakdown and can trigger life-threatening attacks in hereditary angioedema.
E. Metoprolol, a beta-1-selective blocker, does not affect the kinin system.

Objective: Hereditary angioedema is bradykinin-mediated swelling caused by C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency (low C4); ACE inhibitors further reduce bradykinin breakdown and are contraindicated.

Textbook:
**Angioedema** is swelling of the deep dermis, subcutaneous, or submucosal tissue. Separate the two mechanisms before treating.

| Feature | Histamine-mediated (allergic) | Bradykinin-mediated |
|---|---|---|
| Causes | IgE-mediated allergy (foods, drugs, stings), NSAIDs | Hereditary angioedema, acquired C1-INH deficiency, ACE inhibitors, DPP-4 inhibitors, sacubitril |
| Urticaria and itching | Usually present | Absent |
| Onset and duration | Minutes; resolves in hours | Hours; lasts 2 to 5 days |
| Response to epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids | Yes | Poor |
| Acute treatment | Epinephrine, antihistamines, steroids | C1-INH concentrate, icatibant (B2 antagonist), ecallantide (kallikrein inhibitor); fresh frozen plasma if unavailable; secure the airway |

**Hereditary angioedema**
- **Genetics:** autosomal dominant SERPING1 mutations; type 1 (low C1-INH level), type 2 (normal level, low function).
- **Labs:** low C4 (even between attacks), low C1-INH level or function, **normal C1q** (C1q is low in acquired C1-INH deficiency associated with lymphoproliferative disease).
- **Triggers:** trauma, dental and surgical procedures, stress, infection, estrogen (oral contraceptives, pregnancy), ACE inhibitors.
- **Prophylaxis:** lanadelumab (anti-kallikrein antibody), berotralstat, C1-INH concentrate; attenuated androgens (danazol) are older options. Give short-term prophylaxis before dental procedures.

References:
- Busse PJ, et al. US HAEA Medical Advisory Board 2020 Guidelines for the Management of Hereditary Angioedema. J Allergy Clin Immunol Pract. 2021;9:132-150.

Nuggets:
- Avoid ACE inhibitors in hereditary angioedema :: C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency lets kallikrein generate excess bradykinin; ACE normally degrades bradykinin, so ACE inhibition can trigger life-threatening swelling. C4 is low even between attacks.

Topic: Crystal arthropathies

Free: yes

Stem:
A 74-year-old woman comes to the emergency department because of pain and swelling of her right knee for 1 day. She has hypothyroidism and osteoarthritis of both hands. Her temperature is 37.8°C (100°F). The right knee is warm, swollen, and tender, with a large effusion and painful, limited range of motion. An x-ray of the knee shows linear calcification of the menisci and articular cartilage. Arthrocentesis yields cloudy fluid with a leukocyte count of 22,000/mm3 (80% neutrophils); a Gram stain shows no organisms, and a culture is pending.

Lead-in: Polarized light microscopy of the synovial fluid is most likely to show which of the following?

A. Envelope-shaped crystals that do not polarize light
B. Needle-shaped crystals with strong negative birefringence
C. Nonbirefringent spherical aggregates that stain with alizarin red
D. Notched rectangular plates
E. Rhomboid-shaped crystals with weak positive birefringence

Answer: E
Key concept: Calcium pyrophosphate crystals (pseudogout)

Concepts:
A = Calcium oxalate crystals
B = Monosodium urate (gout)
C = Basic calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite)
D = Cholesterol crystals
E = Calcium pyrophosphate (pseudogout)

Explanation:
An acute, warm, inflammatory monoarthritis of the **knee in an older woman**, with **chondrocalcinosis** (linear calcification of the menisci and hyaline cartilage) on x-ray and inflammatory synovial fluid, is **acute calcium pyrophosphate (CPP) crystal arthritis**, also called **pseudogout**.

Under compensated polarized light, CPP crystals are **rhomboid or rod-shaped and weakly positively birefringent** (blue when parallel to the compensator axis). Neutrophils phagocytose the crystals and activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, releasing IL-1 beta and producing the acute inflammation, much as in gout.

Even when crystals are found, **septic arthritis must be excluded with culture**, because crystals and infection can coexist.

Associations worth recognizing (and screening for in younger patients): **hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatasia**, and hypothyroidism, plus older age and osteoarthritis.

Option explanations:
A. Envelope-shaped (dihydrate) calcium oxalate crystals are found in urine with ethylene glycol poisoning and occasionally in joints of patients on long-term dialysis; they are not the cause of chondrocalcinosis.
B. Needle-shaped, strongly negatively birefringent monosodium urate crystals (yellow when parallel) are gout, which classically affects the first metatarsophalangeal joint and does not cause chondrocalcinosis.
C. Basic calcium phosphate (hydroxyapatite) crystals are too small to see individually, are not birefringent, and stain with alizarin red; they cause calcific tendinitis and destructive Milwaukee shoulder.
D. Flat, notched rectangular cholesterol crystals appear in chronic effusions, such as long-standing rheumatoid arthritis.
E. Correct. Rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent calcium pyrophosphate crystals are diagnostic of pseudogout.

Objective: Acute calcium pyrophosphate crystal arthritis (pseudogout) typically affects the knee of older adults, shows chondrocalcinosis on x-ray, and has rhomboid, weakly positively birefringent crystals; associations include hyperparathyroidism, hemochromatosis, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatasia, and hypothyroidism.

**Treatment of acute crystal arthritis:** NSAIDs, colchicine, or glucocorticoids (intra-articular or systemic). Gout also has urate-lowering therapy (allopurinol, febuxostat, probenecid, pegloticase) started for recurrent attacks, tophi, or erosions. There is no equivalent crystal-dissolving therapy for CPP disease; treat associated conditions.

**Synovial fluid leukocyte counts:** normal under 200/mm3; noninflammatory (osteoarthritis) 200 to 2000; inflammatory (crystals, rheumatoid arthritis) 2000 to 50,000; septic usually over 50,000 with neutrophil predominance (overlap occurs).

Discipline: Gross Anatomy & Embryology

Topic: Brachial plexus injuries
Difficulty: 2
Tags: birth injury, shoulder dystocia, nerve injury

Stem:
A 3900-g (8-lb 10-oz) newborn boy is examined 1 hour after delivery. The delivery was complicated by shoulder dystocia, and strong lateral traction was applied to the head and neck to deliver the right shoulder. His right upper extremity hangs limply at his side with the shoulder adducted and internally rotated, the elbow extended, and the forearm pronated. The Moro reflex is absent on the right, but grasp reflexes are present and symmetric. There is no ptosis or miosis.

Lead-in: Injury to which of the following structures is most likely?

A. Lower trunk of the brachial plexus
B. Medial cord of the brachial plexus
C. Posterior cord of the brachial plexus
D. Radial nerve at the spiral groove
E. Upper trunk of the brachial plexus

Answer: E
Key concept: Erb-Duchenne palsy (C5-C6)

Explanation:
Lateral traction that **widens the angle between the neck and shoulder** stretches the **upper trunk of the brachial plexus (C5-C6)**. The resulting **Erb-Duchenne palsy** produces the "waiter's tip" posture:

| Lost muscle action | Muscles (nerve) | Resulting posture |
|---|---|---|
| Shoulder abduction | Deltoid (axillary), supraspinatus (suprascapular) | Arm adducted at the side |
| Lateral rotation | Infraspinatus (suprascapular), teres minor (axillary) | Arm internally rotated |
| Elbow flexion and supination | Biceps, brachialis (musculocutaneous) | Elbow extended, forearm pronated |

The Moro reflex requires shoulder abduction, so it is absent on the affected side, while the **grasp reflex is preserved** because hand muscles are supplied by C8-T1. The absence of Horner syndrome also argues against a lower root (T1) injury.

Most Erb palsies are neurapraxias that recover over weeks to months with physical therapy to prevent contractures; persistent deficits beyond about 3 to 6 months prompt nerve surgery referral.

Option explanations:
A. Lower trunk (C8-T1) injury, Klumpke palsy, follows upward traction on the arm (arm delivered first above the head, or grabbing a branch while falling). It causes a claw hand with loss of the grasp reflex and may cause Horner syndrome.
B. The medial cord supplies the ulnar nerve and part of the median nerve; injury affects hand intrinsic muscles and grasp, which are preserved here.
C. Posterior cord injury affects the axillary and radial nerves: weak shoulder abduction plus wrist drop and loss of elbow and wrist extension. Elbow flexion and supination would be spared.
D. Radial nerve injury at the spiral groove (midshaft humerus fracture, "Saturday night palsy") causes wrist drop with preserved shoulder and elbow flexion.
E. Correct. Upper trunk (C5-C6) injury causes the adducted, internally rotated, extended, pronated "waiter's tip" arm.

Objective: Erb palsy (C5-C6 upper trunk) from lateral neck traction produces an adducted, internally rotated arm with an extended elbow and pronated forearm ("waiter's tip"); the grasp reflex is preserved.

Exam: Step 1
System: Dermatology
Discipline: Immunology
Competency: Causes & mechanisms
Category: Infectious, immunologic & inflammatory disorders
Topic: Autoimmune blistering diseases
Difficulty: 3
Tags: bullae, autoantibodies, elderly

Stem:
An 81-year-old man comes to the office because of itchy blisters on his abdomen, thighs, and the flexor surfaces of his forearms for 3 weeks. He has Parkinson disease treated with carbidopa-levodopa. Examination shows multiple tense bullae 1 to 3 cm in diameter on erythematous, urticarial plaques. The oral mucosa is not involved. Gentle lateral pressure on the skin adjacent to a bulla does not cause the epidermis to separate. A biopsy specimen shows a subepidermal blister containing numerous eosinophils.

Lead-in: Direct immunofluorescence of perilesional skin is most likely to show autoantibodies directed against which of the following?

A. Desmoglein 1 and desmoglein 3
B. Double-stranded DNA
C. Epidermal transglutaminase
D. Hemidesmosomal proteins BP180 and BP230
E. Type VII collagen

Answer: D
Key concept: Bullous pemphigoid

Concepts:
A = Pemphigus vulgaris
B = Systemic lupus erythematosus
C = Dermatitis herpetiformis
D = Bullous pemphigoid
E = Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita

Explanation:
An elderly patient with **intensely pruritic, tense bullae** on an urticarial base, **sparing of the oral mucosa**, a **negative Nikolsky sign**, and a **subepidermal blister rich in eosinophils** has **bullous pemphigoid**, the most common autoimmune blistering disease. Neurologic disease such as Parkinson disease, dementia, or stroke is a known risk factor.

Bullous pemphigoid is a **type II hypersensitivity** reaction. IgG autoantibodies target **hemidesmosomal proteins BP180 (type XVII collagen) and BP230**, which anchor basal keratinocytes to the basement membrane. Complement activation and recruitment of eosinophils and neutrophils release proteases that cleave the dermal-epidermal junction, so the **entire epidermis lifts off as the blister roof**. Because the roof is full thickness, the blisters are **tense** and do not rupture easily, and lateral pressure does not shear the epidermis (Nikolsky negative).

Direct immunofluorescence shows **linear IgG and C3 along the basement membrane zone**.

Option explanations:
A. Anti-desmoglein 1 and 3 antibodies cause pemphigus vulgaris: loss of keratinocyte cohesion within the epidermis (acantholysis), flaccid blisters and erosions, oral involvement, a positive Nikolsky sign, and a net-like intercellular immunofluorescence pattern.
B. Anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies are specific for systemic lupus erythematosus; the lupus band test shows granular immunoglobulin deposits at the dermal-epidermal junction, but SLE does not cause this blistering picture.
C. Antibodies against epidermal transglutaminase cause dermatitis herpetiformis: very itchy grouped vesicles on extensor surfaces associated with celiac disease, with granular IgA in the dermal papillae.
D. Correct. IgG against hemidesmosomal BP180 and BP230 separates the epidermis from the basement membrane, producing tense subepidermal bullae.
E. Antibodies against type VII collagen (anchoring fibrils) cause epidermolysis bullosa acquisita, which presents with trauma-induced blisters on extensor surfaces that heal with scarring and milia.

Objective: Bullous pemphigoid (elderly, tense pruritic bullae, Nikolsky negative, oral sparing, subepidermal blister with eosinophils) is caused by IgG against hemidesmosomal BP180 and BP230, producing linear IgG and C3 at the dermal-epidermal junction.

### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1035
Exam: Step 1
System: Endocrine
Discipline: Physiology
Competency: Formulating the diagnosis
Category: Parathyroid & calcium disorders
Topic: Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia
Difficulty: 5
Tags: hypercalcemia, calcium-sensing receptor, parathyroid
Daily: yes

Stem:
A 34-year-old woman comes to the office for follow-up after routine laboratory studies showed a serum calcium concentration of 10.9 mg/dL. She feels well and has no history of kidney stones, fractures, abdominal pain, or depression. She takes no medications or supplements. Her mother and maternal uncle have been told they have "high calcium," and both are healthy. Examination shows no abnormalities. Repeat laboratory studies show:

Serum calcium: 10.8 mg/dL
Serum phosphorus: 3.0 mg/dL
Serum parathyroid hormone: 58 pg/mL (N=10-65)
Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D: 34 ng/mL
Serum creatinine: 0.8 mg/dL
24-hour urine calcium: 52 mg (N=100-300)
Calcium/creatinine clearance ratio: 0.006

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most likely cause of this patient's hypercalcemia?

A. Ectopic secretion of parathyroid hormone-related peptide
B. Excessive vitamin D intake
C. Inactivating mutation of the calcium-sensing receptor
D. Parathyroid adenoma
E. Parathyroid carcinoma

Answer: C
Key concept: Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia

Concepts:
A = Humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy
B = Vitamin D toxicity
C = Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia
D = Primary hyperparathyroidism
E = Parathyroid carcinoma

Explanation:
Reason in two steps.

**Step 1: Is the hypercalcemia PTH-dependent?** With a calcium of 10.8 mg/dL, a normal parathyroid gland should suppress PTH. A PTH of 58 pg/mL is **inappropriately normal**, so the hypercalcemia is PTH-mediated. That excludes PTH-related peptide, vitamin D excess, granulomas, and osteolysis, all of which suppress PTH. The realistic choices are **primary hyperparathyroidism** and **familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH)**.

**Step 2: What is the kidney doing with calcium?** In primary hyperparathyroidism, the filtered calcium load overwhelms the kidney and urinary calcium is normal or high (calcium/creatinine clearance ratio usually over 0.02). This patient's **urinary calcium is low and the clearance ratio is 0.006 (under 0.01)**, which is the hallmark of **FHH**.

FHH is caused by a heterozygous **inactivating mutation of the calcium-sensing receptor (CaSR)**, inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern (mother and uncle affected). The receptor is less sensitive:
- In the **parathyroid**, a higher calcium is needed to suppress PTH, so the set point shifts upward and PTH stays normal or mildly high.
- In the **thick ascending limb**, CaSR normally inhibits calcium reabsorption when calcium is high; a defective receptor fails to do this, so the kidney keeps reabsorbing calcium and the urine is low in calcium.

FHH is **benign and lifelong**. It does not cause stones, fractures, or end-organ damage, and **parathyroidectomy does not correct it**. Recognizing it prevents unnecessary neck surgery; CASR genetic testing confirms it.

Option explanations:
A. PTH-related peptide (squamous cell carcinomas, renal and breast carcinoma) causes hypercalcemia with suppressed PTH, low phosphorus, and usually symptomatic, rapidly rising calcium.
B. Vitamin D toxicity suppresses PTH and causes hypercalciuria. She takes no supplements, and her 25-hydroxyvitamin D level is normal.
C. Correct. An inactivating CaSR mutation raises the PTH set point and increases renal calcium reabsorption, producing mild hypercalcemia with inappropriately normal PTH and low urinary calcium.
D. A parathyroid adenoma (primary hyperparathyroidism) also gives high calcium with normal or high PTH, but urinary calcium is normal or increased, and the calcium/creatinine clearance ratio is usually above 0.02.
E. Parathyroid carcinoma causes severe hypercalcemia (often over 14 mg/dL), markedly elevated PTH, and a palpable neck mass.

Objective: FHH (autosomal dominant inactivating CASR mutation) causes lifelong mild hypercalcemia with inappropriately normal PTH and low urinary calcium (calcium/creatinine clearance ratio under 0.01); it needs no surgery, which distinguishes it from primary hyperparathyroidism.

Textbook:
**Approach to hypercalcemia**

1. Confirm (correct for albumin, or measure ionized calcium).
2. Measure **PTH**.

| Category | PTH | Examples | Other clues |
|---|---|---|---|
| PTH-dependent | High or inappropriately normal | Primary hyperparathyroidism, FHH, lithium, tertiary hyperparathyroidism | Urine calcium separates primary HPT (normal or high) from FHH (low) |
| PTH-independent | Suppressed | PTHrP (malignancy), calcitriol excess (granulomas, lymphoma), vitamin D toxicity, osteolytic metastases or myeloma, milk-alkali syndrome, thiazides, hyperthyroidism, immobilization | Measure PTHrP, 25-OH and 1,25-OH vitamin D, SPEP |

**Calcium/creatinine clearance ratio** = (urine Ca x serum Cr) / (serum Ca x urine Cr). Under 0.01 favors FHH; over 0.02 favors primary hyperparathyroidism.

**Primary hyperparathyroidism:** usually a single adenoma; "stones, bones, groans, thrones, and psychiatric overtones"; low phosphorus; surgery if symptomatic or if criteria are met (calcium more than 1 mg/dL above normal, osteoporosis, stones or hypercalciuria, eGFR under 60, age under 50).

**Homozygous CASR inactivation** causes neonatal severe hyperparathyroidism, a life-threatening condition.

- Hypercalcemia + low urine calcium = FHH, not an adenoma :: Desensitized calcium-sensing receptors in parathyroid and kidney reset the set point: PTH stays normal and urinary calcium is low (calcium/creatinine clearance ratio under 0.01). It is benign; do not send the patient for parathyroidectomy.

### QUESTION

Exam: Step 1
System: Female Reproductive
Discipline: Pathology
Competency: Prognosis & outcome
Category: Menstrual & endocrine disorders
Topic: Polycystic ovary syndrome
Difficulty: 2
Tags: anovulation, hyperandrogenism, unopposed estrogen
Free: yes

Stem:
A 27-year-old woman comes to the office because of irregular menstrual periods since menarche; she has 4 or 5 periods each year. She also has acne and dark, coarse hair on her upper lip and chin. A urine pregnancy test is negative. Her BMI is 34 kg/m2. Examination shows terminal hair on the chin, upper lip, and periareolar skin, and velvety, hyperpigmented skin on the back of the neck. Pelvic examination shows no abnormalities. Serum total testosterone concentration is mildly increased; prolactin, TSH, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone concentrations are within normal limits. Transvaginal ultrasonography shows enlarged ovaries, each with more than 20 peripheral follicles.

Lead-in: Without treatment, this patient is at greatest risk for which of the following?

A. Breast carcinoma
B. Cervical squamous cell carcinoma
C. Endometrial carcinoma
D. Ovarian serous carcinoma
E. Vulvar squamous cell carcinoma

Answer: C
Key concept: PCOS increases endometrial cancer risk through unopposed estrogen

Concepts:
A = Breast carcinoma
B = HPV-related cervical carcinoma
C = Endometrial carcinoma
D = Ovarian epithelial carcinoma
E = Vulvar carcinoma

Explanation:
**Oligomenorrhea since menarche, clinical and biochemical hyperandrogenism** (hirsutism, acne, high testosterone), **polycystic ovarian morphology**, obesity, and **acanthosis nigricans** (a marker of insulin resistance), with other causes excluded (normal prolactin, TSH, and 17-hydroxyprogesterone), is **polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)**.

The core pathophysiology is a self-reinforcing loop:
- **Insulin resistance** causes hyperinsulinemia, which stimulates ovarian theca cells to make androgens and lowers hepatic sex hormone-binding globulin (more free testosterone).
- **Increased GnRH pulse frequency** favors LH over FSH (LH:FSH ratio often over 2); LH drives theca androgen production, while relatively low FSH prevents follicles from maturing.

The consequence that matters most here is **chronic anovulation**. Without ovulation there is no corpus luteum and therefore **no progesterone**. The endometrium is exposed to continuous estrogen (including estrone made from androgens in adipose tissue) without the cyclic progesterone that would stabilize and shed it. **Unopposed estrogen** causes endometrial hyperplasia, which can progress to **endometrial carcinoma** (endometrioid type), sometimes at a young age.

Protection: combined hormonal contraceptives, cyclic progestins, or a levonorgestrel IUD, plus weight loss and treatment of insulin resistance.

Option explanations:
A. PCOS has not been consistently shown to raise breast cancer risk substantially; the strong, well-established association is with endometrial cancer.
B. Cervical squamous cell carcinoma is driven by persistent high-risk HPV infection, not by hormonal status.
C. Correct. Chronic anovulation leaves estrogen unopposed by progesterone, causing endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma.
D. Ovarian epithelial cancer risk is lowered by fewer ovulations (pregnancy, oral contraceptives); PCOS is not a major risk factor for high-grade serous carcinoma.
E. Vulvar squamous cell carcinoma arises from HPV infection or lichen sclerosus in older women.

Objective: Chronic anovulation in PCOS leaves the endometrium exposed to estrogen without progesterone, increasing the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma; combined hormonal contraceptives or cyclic progestins protect the endometrium.

Textbook:
**Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)** is the most common endocrine disorder of reproductive-age women.

- **Diagnosis (Rotterdam criteria, 2 of 3):** oligo- or anovulation; clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism; polycystic ovaries on ultrasound. Exclude thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, nonclassic congenital adrenal hyperplasia (17-hydroxyprogesterone), Cushing syndrome, and androgen-secreting tumors (rapid virilization, very high testosterone or DHEA-S).
- **Labs:** increased LH:FSH ratio, increased free testosterone, decreased SHBG, insulin resistance.
- **Associated risks:** endometrial hyperplasia and carcinoma, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease, obstructive sleep apnea, infertility, depression.

**Treatment by goal:**
- First-line for all with overweight: lifestyle change and weight loss (5% to 10% can restore ovulation).
- Cycle control and endometrial protection: combined oral contraceptives (also reduce androgens); progestins if estrogen is contraindicated.
- Hirsutism: combined oral contraceptives, spironolactone (with contraception), eflornithine cream, laser.
- Infertility: letrozole (first-line ovulation induction), clomiphene, gonadotropins, in vitro fertilization.
- Metabolic: metformin for insulin resistance or prediabetes.

References:

ID: AQ-1037
Exam: Step 1
System: Female Reproductive
Discipline: Genetics
Competency: Causes & mechanisms
Category: Structural & congenital disorders
Topic: Disorders of sex development
Difficulty: 3
Tags: primary amenorrhea, 46XY, androgen receptor

Stem:
A 16-year-old girl is brought to the physician because she has not had a menstrual period. She has been otherwise healthy. She is 172 cm (5 ft 8 in) tall. Breast development is Tanner stage 4; pubic and axillary hair are sparse (Tanner stage 1). The external genitalia are female; the vagina is short and ends blindly. Pelvic ultrasonography shows no uterus or ovaries, and there are bilateral oval structures within the inguinal canals. Serum testosterone concentration is in the normal adult male range, and serum LH concentration is increased.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most likely cause of this patient's condition?

A. Androgen receptor dysfunction
B. Deficiency of 17-alpha-hydroxylase
C. Deficiency of 5-alpha-reductase type 2
D. Gonadal dysgenesis with a 45,X karyotype
E. Mullerian duct agenesis

Answer: A
Key concept: Complete androgen insensitivity syndrome

Concepts:
A = Androgen insensitivity syndrome
B = 17-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency
C = 5-alpha-reductase deficiency
D = Turner syndrome
E = Mullerian agenesis (MRKH)

Explanation:
Primary amenorrhea with **well-developed breasts** but **scant pubic and axillary hair**, a **blind-ending vagina**, **no uterus**, **inguinal gonads** (testes), **male-range testosterone**, and **high LH** is **complete androgen insensitivity syndrome (CAIS)** in a 46,XY individual.

Walk through each finding from the biology:
- **Testes are present** (SRY on the Y chromosome), so they secrete **anti-Mullerian hormone**, which makes the Mullerian ducts regress: no uterus, fallopian tubes, or upper vagina. The vagina is short because only its lower part (urogenital sinus) forms.
- Testosterone is made normally, but the **androgen receptor (X-linked AR gene) does not work**. Wolffian ducts do not develop, the external genitalia form along the default female pathway, and androgen-dependent **pubic and axillary hair is sparse**.
- Testosterone is **aromatized to estradiol**, which acts on intact estrogen receptors, producing **normal breast development** and female body habitus.
- Androgen resistance at the hypothalamus and pituitary removes negative feedback, so **LH rises**, driving testosterone (and estradiol) even higher.

Individuals with CAIS typically identify as female. Management includes **gonadectomy** (because of germ cell tumor risk, often deferred until after puberty to allow spontaneous breast development), estrogen replacement afterward, vaginal dilation if desired, and psychological support with careful disclosure.

Option explanations:
A. Correct. A nonfunctional androgen receptor in a 46,XY individual explains female external genitalia, absent Mullerian structures, breasts from aromatized testosterone, and sparse androgen-dependent hair.
B. 46,XY 17-alpha-hydroxylase deficiency also produces female-appearing external genitalia, but sex steroids are low, so breasts do not develop, and mineralocorticoid excess causes hypertension and hypokalemia.
C. 5-alpha-reductase deficiency (46,XY) causes ambiguous genitalia at birth and marked virilization at puberty because testosterone itself still acts on the androgen receptor; Wolffian structures develop.
D. Turner syndrome (45,X) causes primary amenorrhea with short stature and absent breast development (streak gonads make no estrogen), and the uterus is present.
E. Mullerian agenesis (Mayer-Rokitansky-Kuster-Hauser syndrome) occurs in 46,XX individuals with normal ovaries, normal female testosterone, and normal pubic and axillary hair; only the uterus and upper vagina are absent.

Objective: Primary amenorrhea with breast development, absent uterus, sparse pubic and axillary hair, inguinal gonads, and male-range testosterone indicates complete androgen insensitivity (46,XY with a nonfunctional androgen receptor).

Textbook:
**Sexual differentiation:** SRY (Y chromosome) leads to testis development. Sertoli cells secrete anti-Mullerian hormone (Mullerian regression). Leydig cells secrete testosterone (Wolffian development into epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles). Dihydrotestosterone (via 5-alpha-reductase) forms the penis, scrotum, and prostate. Without testes, Mullerian ducts develop and external genitalia are female.

| Condition | Karyotype | Breasts | Uterus | Pubic hair | Key labs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Turner syndrome | 45,X | Absent | Present | Normal or sparse | High FSH (hypergonadotropic) |
| Mullerian agenesis | 46,XX | Normal | Absent | Normal | Normal female hormones |
| Complete androgen insensitivity | 46,XY | Normal | Absent | Sparse | Male testosterone, high LH |
| 5-alpha-reductase deficiency | 46,XY | Absent | Absent | Male pattern at puberty | Normal testosterone, low DHT |

Topic: Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy
Difficulty: 3
Tags: eclampsia, seizure, magnesium

Stem:
A 24-year-old primigravid woman at 36 weeks' gestation is brought to the emergency department after a generalized tonic-clonic seizure at home that lasted about 1 minute. Earlier today, she had a severe headache and blurred vision. Her blood pressure was normal at a prenatal visit 3 weeks ago. She is now drowsy but arousable and is not seizing. Her pulse is 98/min, respirations are 18/min, and blood pressure is 172/112 mm Hg. Oxygen saturation is 96% on room air. Examination shows 3+ pitting edema of the lower extremities and hyperreflexia without focal neurologic deficits. Urine dipstick shows 3+ protein. The fetal heart rate tracing shows a baseline of 150/min with moderate variability. She is placed in the left lateral decubitus position, and supplemental oxygen is administered.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. CT scan of the head
B. Emergency cesarean delivery
C. Intravenous lorazepam
D. Intravenous magnesium sulfate
E. Intravenous phenytoin

Answer: D
Key concept: Magnesium sulfate for eclampsia

Explanation:
New-onset hypertension (172/112 mm Hg, a severe range) and proteinuria after 20 weeks' gestation is **preeclampsia**; a **new generalized seizure** with no other cause makes it **eclampsia**. Headache, visual disturbance, and hyperreflexia are warning signs that preceded it.

Priorities after airway, breathing, circulation, and lateral positioning (already done):

1. **Intravenous magnesium sulfate**: a loading dose of 4 to 6 g over 15 to 20 minutes, then 1 to 2 g/h. Magnesium is superior to benzodiazepines and phenytoin for **stopping and preventing recurrent eclamptic seizures** and reduces maternal death. It likely works by cerebral vasodilation and NMDA receptor antagonism.
2. **Treat severe-range blood pressure** within 30 to 60 minutes with intravenous labetalol, intravenous hydralazine, or oral immediate-release nifedipine, to prevent stroke.
3. **Deliver once the mother is stabilized.** Eclampsia is an indication for delivery, but it does not by itself require emergency cesarean; induction is reasonable when the fetal status is reassuring, as here.

Monitor for magnesium toxicity: **loss of deep tendon reflexes first**, then respiratory depression, then cardiac arrest. Check reflexes, respiratory rate, urine output, and renal function (magnesium is renally cleared). The antidote is **calcium gluconate**.

Option explanations:
A. Head imaging is indicated for focal deficits, prolonged coma, seizures that recur despite magnesium, or atypical timing. It should not delay magnesium therapy in typical eclampsia.
B. Delivery is the definitive treatment, but the mother must be stabilized first. With a reassuring fetal tracing, there is no need for an emergency cesarean before magnesium and blood pressure control.
C. Benzodiazepines are reserved for seizures that persist despite magnesium; they are less effective at preventing recurrence and add sedation and respiratory depression.
D. Correct. Intravenous magnesium sulfate is first-line to treat and prevent eclamptic seizures.
E. Phenytoin is inferior to magnesium sulfate for eclampsia.

Objective: Eclampsia is treated with intravenous magnesium sulfate (to stop and prevent seizures) plus urgent control of severe-range blood pressure, followed by delivery once the mother is stabilized.

Textbook:
**Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy**

| Disorder | Definition |
|---|---|
| Chronic hypertension | Hypertension before pregnancy or before 20 weeks |
| Gestational hypertension | New hypertension at or after 20 weeks, no proteinuria or end-organ damage |
| Preeclampsia | New hypertension at or after 20 weeks with proteinuria or end-organ dysfunction |
| Preeclampsia with severe features | BP 160/110 or higher, platelets under 100,000, liver transaminases twice normal or RUQ pain, creatinine over 1.1, pulmonary edema, new headache or visual symptoms |

- **Pathogenesis:** abnormal trophoblast invasion and failed remodeling of spiral arteries; placental ischemia releases antiangiogenic factors (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1) that cause systemic endothelial dysfunction.

### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1039
Exam: Step 1
System: Pregnancy
Discipline: Microbiology
Competency: Causes & mechanisms
Category: Obstetric complications
Topic: Listeriosis in pregnancy
Difficulty: 3
Tags: foodborne infection, intracellular bacteria, neonatal sepsis

Stem:
A 27-year-old woman at 29 weeks' gestation comes to the emergency department because of fever, chills, muscle aches, and mild diarrhea for 2 days. One week ago, she attended a party where she ate deli meat sandwiches and soft cheese made from unpasteurized milk. Her temperature is 38.9°C (102°F), pulse is 104/min, and blood pressure is 112/68 mm Hg. Examination shows no focal findings. The fetal heart rate is 170/min. Blood cultures grow an organism within 24 hours.

Lead-in: Which of the following best describes the most likely causal organism?

A. Gram-negative, comma-shaped rod that grows at 42°C
B. Gram-negative, lactose-fermenting rod
C. Gram-positive, beta-hemolytic coccus in chains that is resistant to bacitracin
D. Gram-positive, coagulase-positive coccus in clusters
E. Gram-positive rod with tumbling motility that grows at refrigerator temperature

Answer: E
Key concept: Listeria monocytogenes

Concepts:
A = Campylobacter jejuni
B = Escherichia coli
C = Streptococcus agalactiae
D = Staphylococcus aureus
E = Listeria monocytogenes

Explanation:
A febrile, flu-like illness with mild GI symptoms in a **pregnant woman** who ate **deli meats and unpasteurized soft cheese**, with **bacteremia** and fetal tachycardia, is **listeriosis**.

**Listeria monocytogenes** is a **gram-positive rod** with two textbook properties:
- **Tumbling motility** at room temperature (from peritrichous flagella).
- **Cold growth**: it multiplies at refrigerator temperatures (4°C), which is why ready-to-eat refrigerated foods such as deli meats, hot dogs, smoked seafood, and soft cheeses are classic sources.

It is a **facultative intracellular** pathogen. Internalin proteins mediate entry into cells, listeriolysin O lets it escape the phagosome, and **ActA polymerizes host actin into "rockets"** that push it directly from cell to cell, avoiding antibodies. This lifestyle explains why it targets people with impaired **cell-mediated immunity**: pregnant women, neonates, older adults, and immunocompromised patients. During pregnancy it **crosses the placenta**, causing chorioamnionitis, preterm labor, stillbirth, or granulomatosis infantiseptica, and later neonatal sepsis and meningitis.

Treatment is **ampicillin** (with or without gentamicin). Listeria is **intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins**, which is why ampicillin is added to empiric meningitis regimens for neonates, adults over 50, and immunocompromised patients.

Option explanations:
A. Campylobacter jejuni is a comma- or S-shaped gram-negative rod that grows at 42°C; it causes bloody diarrhea after undercooked poultry and can trigger Guillain-Barre syndrome, but it rarely causes bacteremia or crosses the placenta.
B. Escherichia coli is a gram-negative lactose fermenter and a common cause of neonatal sepsis and urinary tract infection in pregnancy, but it is not linked to deli meats and soft cheese.
C. Group B streptococcus (Streptococcus agalactiae) is a beta-hemolytic, bacitracin-resistant, CAMP-positive coccus that colonizes the genital tract and causes neonatal sepsis; it is acquired during delivery, not from food.
D. Staphylococcus aureus causes preformed-toxin food poisoning with vomiting within hours, not a febrile bacteremic illness a week after exposure.
E. Correct. Listeria monocytogenes is a cold-tolerant gram-positive rod with tumbling motility that is acquired from deli meats and soft cheeses and can cross the placenta.

Objective: Listeria monocytogenes (gram-positive rod, tumbling motility, grows in the cold) is acquired from deli meats and soft cheeses, crosses the placenta, and is treated with ampicillin; it is intrinsically resistant to cephalosporins.

Textbook:
**Listeria monocytogenes**

- **Microbiology:** gram-positive rod (sometimes in short chains), catalase positive, narrow zone of beta-hemolysis, tumbling motility, grows at 4°C.
- **Virulence:** internalins (cell entry), listeriolysin O (phagosome escape), ActA (actin rockets for cell-to-cell spread).
- **Sources:** deli meats, hot dogs, refrigerated smoked seafood, unpasteurized milk and soft cheeses, raw sprouts, melons.
- **Disease:** self-limited febrile gastroenteritis in healthy adults; bacteremia and meningitis or rhombencephalitis in immunocompromised and older adults; transplacental infection in pregnancy (preterm labor, stillbirth, granulomatosis infantiseptica with disseminated microabscesses); early- and late-onset neonatal sepsis and meningitis.
- **Treatment:** ampicillin plus gentamicin for invasive disease; trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for penicillin allergy. Cephalosporins are ineffective.
- **Prevention in pregnancy:** avoid unpasteurized dairy and soft cheeses made from unpasteurized milk; heat deli meats and hot dogs until steaming.

**Neonatal sepsis and meningitis organisms (first month):** group B streptococcus, E. coli, Listeria. Empiric therapy: ampicillin plus gentamicin, or ampicillin plus cefotaxime.

### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1040
Exam: Step 1
System: Multisystem
Discipline: Pharmacology
Competency: Pharmacotherapy
Category: Toxins & environmental injury
Topic: Toxic alcohols
Difficulty: 3
Tags: anion gap, osmolal gap, antidotes
Free: yes

Stem:
A 46-year-old man is brought to the emergency department 4 hours after his wife found him confused and slurring his words in their garage. He has a history of alcohol use disorder. His pulse is 110/min, respirations are 28/min and deep, and blood pressure is 142/88 mm Hg. He is somnolent but arousable; there is no odor of alcohol on his breath. Laboratory studies show:

Serum Na+: 140 mEq/L
Serum Cl-: 100 mEq/L
Serum HCO3-: 8 mEq/L
Serum urea nitrogen: 18 mg/dL
Serum glucose: 92 mg/dL
Serum creatinine: 1.9 mg/dL (baseline 0.9 mg/dL)
Measured serum osmolality: 336 mOsm/kg H2O
Serum ethanol: undetectable

Urinalysis shows numerous envelope-shaped crystals.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most appropriate pharmacotherapy?

A. Activated charcoal
B. Deferoxamine
C. Flumazenil
D. Fomepizole
E. Methylene blue

Answer: D
Key concept: Ethylene glycol poisoning treated with fomepizole

Explanation:
Work the numbers first:
- **Anion gap** = 140 - (100 + 8) = **32**: a high anion gap metabolic acidosis (with deep Kussmaul respirations).
- **Calculated osmolality** = 2(140) + 92/18 + 18/2.8 ≈ 291 mOsm/kg. **Osmolal gap** = 336 - 291 = **45**, markedly increased. An unmeasured, osmotically active substance is present, and ethanol is undetectable.

High anion gap plus high osmolal gap means a **toxic alcohol**. **Acute kidney injury** and **envelope-shaped (calcium oxalate dihydrate) crystals** identify **ethylene glycol**, found in antifreeze, which is often stored in garages.

Ethylene glycol itself is relatively nontoxic. **Alcohol dehydrogenase** converts it to glycoaldehyde, then glycolic acid (the main cause of the acidosis), glyoxylic acid, and **oxalic acid**, which precipitates with calcium as oxalate crystals in the kidney (acute tubular injury) and can cause **hypocalcemia**.

**Fomepizole** competitively inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase and stops formation of the toxic acids. Severe acidosis, kidney failure, or high levels also call for **hemodialysis**, which removes both the parent alcohol and its acids. Thiamine and pyridoxine shunt glyoxylic acid toward less toxic metabolites. Ethanol (a competing ADH substrate) is an alternative when fomepizole is unavailable.

Option explanations:
A. Activated charcoal does not meaningfully bind small alcohols like ethylene glycol, and absorption is complete hours after ingestion.
B. Deferoxamine chelates iron in iron poisoning, which can cause a high anion gap acidosis but presents with GI bleeding, not oxalate crystals or an osmolal gap.
C. Flumazenil reverses benzodiazepines; it can precipitate seizures in chronic users and does not treat acidosis.
D. Correct. Fomepizole inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase and prevents ethylene glycol from being converted to glycolic and oxalic acids.
E. Methylene blue treats methemoglobinemia.

Objective: A high anion gap metabolic acidosis with a large osmolal gap, acute kidney injury, and calcium oxalate crystals indicates ethylene glycol poisoning; fomepizole blocks alcohol dehydrogenase and prevents formation of toxic acids.

| Alcohol | Source | Toxic metabolite | Signature findings | Treatment |

| Methanol | Windshield fluid, moonshine, fuel | Formic acid | Visual blurring, "snowfield" vision, optic disc hyperemia, blindness; basal ganglia necrosis | Fomepizole, hemodialysis, folate |

**Osmolal gap** = measured osmolality - (2 Na + glucose/18 + BUN/2.8). A gap over about 10 suggests toxic alcohols, ethanol, mannitol, or propylene glycol. Early after ingestion the osmolal gap is high and the anion gap normal; as metabolism proceeds, the osmolal gap falls and the anion gap rises.
