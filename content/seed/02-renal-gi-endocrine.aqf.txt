### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1013
Exam: Step 2 CK
System: Gastrointestinal
Discipline: Internal Medicine
Competency: Pharmacotherapy
Category: Infectious & inflammatory disorders
Topic: Clostridioides difficile infection
Difficulty: 2
Tags: diarrhea, antibiotics, hospital-acquired

Stem:
A 72-year-old woman is evaluated in the hospital because of watery diarrhea with 7 to 8 bowel movements daily and crampy lower abdominal pain for 3 days. Ten days ago, she was admitted for pyelonephritis and treated with intravenous ceftriaxone, which was changed to oral cefpodoxime 4 days ago. Her temperature is 38.1°C (100.6°F), pulse is 96/min, and blood pressure is 128/76 mm Hg. The abdomen is soft and mildly tender in both lower quadrants without distention or rebound tenderness. Bowel sounds are present. Leukocyte count is 13,800/mm3, and serum creatinine concentration is 0.9 mg/dL (baseline 0.8 mg/dL). A stool nucleic acid amplification test for toxigenic Clostridioides difficile is positive. Cefpodoxime is discontinued.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most appropriate pharmacotherapy?

A. Intravenous vancomycin
B. Oral metronidazole
C. Oral vancomycin
D. Probiotic therapy with Saccharomyces boulardii
E. Rifaximin

Answer: C
Key concept: Oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin for C. difficile

Explanation:
Diarrhea (three or more unformed stools in 24 hours) after cephalosporin therapy in a hospitalized older adult, with a positive toxigenic C. difficile test, is **Clostridioides difficile infection**. Her leukocyte count is under 15,000/mm3 and her creatinine is under 1.5 mg/dL, so this is a **nonsevere initial episode**. There are no fulminant features (no hypotension, shock, ileus, or megacolon).

Current IDSA/SHEA guidance for an initial episode, nonsevere or severe:
- **Fidaxomicin** 200 mg twice daily for 10 days (preferred where available because it lowers recurrence), or
- **Oral vancomycin** 125 mg four times daily for 10 days.

Of the choices offered, **oral vancomycin** is correct. The route matters: vancomycin is a large glycopeptide that is **not absorbed from the gut**, which is exactly why it works orally (it stays in the colon at high concentration), and why intravenous vancomycin fails (it is not secreted into the bowel lumen).

Stopping the inciting antibiotic, as was done here, is also part of treatment.

Option explanations:
A. Intravenous vancomycin does not reach the colonic lumen and has no role in treating C. difficile infection.
B. Oral metronidazole is no longer first-line because of higher failure and recurrence rates; it is reserved for nonsevere disease when fidaxomicin and vancomycin are unavailable.
C. Correct. Oral vancomycin (or fidaxomicin) is first-line for an initial episode of C. difficile infection.
D. Probiotics have not been shown to treat active C. difficile infection and are not recommended as therapy.
E. Rifaximin is occasionally used as a "chaser" after vancomycin for multiply recurrent disease, not as initial therapy.

Objective: An initial episode of Clostridioides difficile infection is treated with oral fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin; intravenous vancomycin does not reach the colon, and metronidazole is no longer first-line.

Textbook:
**Clostridioides difficile** is a gram-positive, spore-forming, anaerobic rod. Toxin A (enterotoxin) and toxin B (cytotoxin) disrupt the actin cytoskeleton and cause colitis with pseudomembranes.

- **Risk factors:** antibiotics (clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, broad-spectrum penicillins), hospitalization or long-term care, age over 65, proton pump inhibitors, immunosuppression.
- **Presentation:** watery diarrhea, lower abdominal cramps, fever, leukocytosis (sometimes very high). Fulminant disease: hypotension, ileus, toxic megacolon.
- **Testing:** test only unformed stool from patients with three or more unformed stools in 24 hours; NAAT or a multistep algorithm with toxin enzyme immunoassay. Do not perform a test of cure.

| Episode | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Initial, nonsevere or severe (WBC over 15,000 or creatinine over 1.5 mg/dL) | Fidaxomicin, or oral vancomycin, for 10 days |
| Fulminant (hypotension, shock, ileus, megacolon) | Oral vancomycin 500 mg four times daily plus IV metronidazole; rectal vancomycin if ileus; surgical consultation |
| First recurrence | Fidaxomicin, or vancomycin tapered and pulsed; consider bezlotoxumab |
| Multiple recurrences | Fecal microbiota transplantation |

- **Infection control:** contact precautions; wash hands with **soap and water** (alcohol does not kill spores); clean surfaces with sporicidal agents.

References:
- Johnson S, et al. Clinical Practice Guideline by IDSA and SHEA: 2021 Focused Update Guidelines on Management of Clostridioides difficile Infection in Adults. Clin Infect Dis. 2021;73:e1029-e1044.

Nuggets:
- C. difficile: oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin, not metronidazole :: Current guidelines dropped metronidazole as first-line. Vancomycin must be given orally because intravenous vancomycin is not secreted into the colon.

### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1018
Exam: Step 2 CK
System: Endocrine
Discipline: Internal Medicine
Competency: Mixed management
Category: Diabetes mellitus & endocrine pancreas
Topic: Diabetic ketoacidosis
Difficulty: 4
Tags: DKA, potassium, insulin

Stem:
A 19-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes mellitus is brought to the emergency department because of nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and increased thirst for 2 days. She ran out of insulin 4 days ago. Her pulse is 124/min, respirations are 30/min and deep, and blood pressure is 98/62 mm Hg. Her mucous membranes are dry. Laboratory studies show:

Serum Na+: 131 mEq/L
Serum K+: 3.0 mEq/L
Serum Cl-: 96 mEq/L
Serum HCO3-: 9 mEq/L
Serum glucose: 520 mg/dL
Serum creatinine: 1.4 mg/dL
Serum beta-hydroxybutyrate: markedly increased
Arterial pH: 7.08

An ECG shows flattened T waves and prominent U waves. An intravenous infusion of 0.9% saline is started.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. Begin a continuous intravenous infusion of regular insulin
B. Begin intravenous potassium chloride and delay insulin until serum potassium is at least 3.3 mEq/L
C. Give intravenous sodium bicarbonate
D. Give subcutaneous insulin glargine
E. Switch the intravenous fluid to 5% dextrose in 0.45% saline

Answer: B
Key concept: Replace potassium before insulin when K+ is under 3.3 in DKA

Explanation:
This patient has **diabetic ketoacidosis**: glucose 520 mg/dL, anion gap 131 - (96 + 9) = 26, pH 7.08, and strongly positive ketones. Fluids have been started, which is always first.

The next decision hinges on **potassium**. In DKA, **total-body potassium is severely depleted** (osmotic diuresis, vomiting, and secondary hyperaldosteronism waste it), even though the serum value is often normal or high at presentation because insulin deficiency and hyperosmolality shift potassium out of cells.

**Insulin drives potassium back into cells** by stimulating the Na+/K+-ATPase. Starting insulin when the serum potassium is already **3.0 mEq/L** (with U waves on the ECG) would push it dangerously lower and risk ventricular arrhythmias, respiratory muscle weakness, and cardiac arrest.

Guideline rule:
- **K+ under 3.3 mEq/L: hold insulin and give potassium (about 20 to 40 mEq/h) until K+ reaches 3.3 or more.**
- K+ 3.3 to 5.2: give insulin and add potassium to the fluids.
- K+ over 5.2: give insulin, recheck potassium every 2 hours, no supplementation yet.

Option explanations:
A. Insulin is essential to close the anion gap, but starting it at a potassium of 3.0 mEq/L can precipitate life-threatening hypokalemia.
B. Correct. When potassium is under 3.3 mEq/L, replace potassium first and delay insulin.
C. Bicarbonate is not indicated unless pH is below about 6.9; it can worsen hypokalemia and has not been shown to improve outcomes.
D. Subcutaneous basal insulin is started near the end of treatment, overlapping the drip by 1 to 2 hours once the anion gap closes; it is not initial therapy for DKA.
E. Dextrose is added when glucose falls to about 200 to 250 mg/dL so that insulin can continue until ketosis resolves. Glucose here is 520 mg/dL.

Objective: In diabetic ketoacidosis, total-body potassium is depleted; if serum potassium is below 3.3 mEq/L, replace potassium and delay insulin, which would otherwise shift potassium into cells and precipitate arrhythmias.

Textbook:
**Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** results from absolute or relative insulin deficiency plus counter-regulatory hormone excess (glucagon, cortisol, catecholamines).

- **Pathophysiology:** unrestrained lipolysis delivers fatty acids to the liver, where glucagon-driven beta-oxidation produces ketone bodies (beta-hydroxybutyrate, acetoacetate), causing an anion gap acidosis. Hyperglycemia causes osmotic diuresis and volume depletion.
- **Triggers:** missed insulin, infection, myocardial infarction, pancreatitis, new-onset type 1 diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitors (euglycemic DKA).
- **Presentation:** polyuria, polydipsia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, Kussmaul respirations, fruity breath, altered mental status.
- **Labs:** glucose usually over 250 mg/dL, pH under 7.3, bicarbonate under 18, anion gap over 12, ketones. Correct sodium for hyperglycemia (add about 1.6 to 2.4 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL of glucose above 100).

**Management sequence:**
1. Isotonic saline (1 to 1.5 L in the first hour).
2. Check potassium **before** insulin (see rule above).
3. Regular insulin infusion (0.1 units/kg/h).
4. Add dextrose when glucose reaches about 200 mg/dL; continue insulin until the anion gap closes.
5. Overlap subcutaneous basal insulin 1 to 2 hours before stopping the infusion.
6. Monitor phosphate and magnesium; treat the precipitating cause.

**Complications:** hypokalemia, hypoglycemia, cerebral edema (especially in children, from overly rapid correction of osmolality), mucormycosis.

| Feature | DKA | Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state |
|---|---|---|
| Typical patient | Type 1 diabetes, younger | Type 2 diabetes, older |
| Glucose | Usually 250 to 600 | Often over 600 |
| Ketones, pH | Positive, acidotic | Minimal, pH over 7.3 |
| Osmolality | Variable | Over 320 mOsm/kg, often with coma |

References:
- Umpierrez GE, et al. Hyperglycemic Crises in Adults With Diabetes: A Consensus Report. Diabetes Care. 2024;47:1257-1275.

Nuggets:
- DKA depletes total-body potassium even when the serum level looks normal :: Insulin deficiency shifts K+ out of cells while osmotic diuresis wastes it. Once insulin starts, potassium plunges, so replace K+ first if it is below 3.3 mEq/L.
