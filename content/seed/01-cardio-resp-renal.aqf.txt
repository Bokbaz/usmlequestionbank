Exam: Step 1
System: Cardiovascular
Discipline: Pathology
Competency: Formulating the diagnosis
Category: Ischemic heart disease
Topic: Complications of myocardial infarction
Difficulty: 3
Tags: murmur, myocardial infarction, mitral regurgitation
Free: yes

Stem:
A 64-year-old man is brought to the emergency department because of sudden-onset severe shortness of breath that began 2 hours ago. Four days ago, he was admitted to another hospital for an inferior wall myocardial infarction and was treated with aspirin, clopidogrel, and atorvastatin; he declined cardiac catheterization. He has hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus. His temperature is 37°C (98.6°F), pulse is 118/min, respirations are 30/min, and blood pressure is 88/54 mm Hg. Oxygen saturation is 84% on room air. Crackles are heard over both lung fields. Cardiac examination shows a new grade 3/6 holosystolic murmur that is loudest at the apex and radiates to the left axilla. There is no jugular venous distention. An ECG shows Q waves in leads II, III, and aVF.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most likely cause of this patient's current condition?

A. Aortic valve leaflet perforation
B. Interventricular septal rupture
C. Left ventricular free wall rupture
D. Papillary muscle rupture
E. Right ventricular infarction

Answer: D
Key concept: Papillary muscle rupture

Explanation:
This patient developed **flash pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock with a new apical holosystolic murmur radiating to the axilla, 4 days after an inferior MI**. That combination is acute mitral regurgitation from **papillary muscle rupture**.

Why the inferior MI matters: the **posteromedial papillary muscle has a single blood supply** (the posterior descending artery, usually from the right coronary artery), so it infarcts and ruptures far more often than the anterolateral muscle, which receives blood from both the LAD (diagonal branches) and the left circumflex.

Why the lungs flood so fast: in acute mitral regurgitation the left atrium has had no time to dilate. A small, stiff atrium receiving a large regurgitant volume generates a sudden rise in left atrial and pulmonary capillary pressure, producing pulmonary edema, while forward stroke volume falls and the patient becomes hypotensive.

Rupture typically occurs **2 to 7 days** after infarction, when macrophages are clearing necrotic myocardium and the tissue is at its weakest. Echocardiography confirms the diagnosis; management is afterload reduction, often an intra-aortic balloon pump, and emergency surgical repair or replacement.

Option explanations:
A. Aortic leaflet perforation is a complication of infective endocarditis. It produces aortic regurgitation: an early diastolic decrescendo murmur at the left sternal border, not an apical holosystolic murmur.
B. Septal rupture also appears 3 to 5 days after MI with a new holosystolic murmur and shock, but the murmur is loudest at the left lower sternal border (often with a thrill), and right heart catheterization shows an oxygen step-up from the right atrium to the right ventricle. An apical murmur radiating to the axilla localizes to the mitral valve.
C. Free wall rupture (usually 5 to 14 days after MI) causes hemopericardium and tamponade: hypotension, jugular venous distention, muffled heart sounds, and often pulseless electrical activity. It does not cause a new murmur, and this patient has no jugular venous distention.
D. Correct. Acute mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle rupture explains the apical holosystolic murmur, pulmonary edema, and hypotension a few days after an inferior MI.
E. Right ventricular infarction complicates inferior MI at the time of the infarct, causing hypotension with jugular venous distention and clear lungs. It does not produce a murmur or pulmonary edema.

Objective: A new apical holosystolic murmur with flash pulmonary edema 2 to 7 days after a myocardial infarction (classically inferior) indicates papillary muscle rupture causing acute mitral regurgitation.

Textbook:
Complications of myocardial infarction follow the healing timeline of the infarct. Knowing **when** each complication appears is often enough to answer the question.

| Time after MI | Complication | Distinguishing clue |
|---|---|---|
| Minutes to 24 h | Ventricular arrhythmia (most common cause of early death), cardiogenic shock | Sudden death, hypotension |
| First 24 h, inferior MI | Right ventricular infarction | Hypotension, JVD, clear lungs; avoid nitrates, give fluids |
| 1 to 3 days | Fibrinous (peri-infarction) pericarditis | Pleuritic pain, friction rub |
| 2 to 7 days | Papillary muscle rupture | New apical holosystolic murmur, flash pulmonary edema |
| 3 to 5 days | Interventricular septal rupture | New left sternal border murmur, O2 step-up RA to RV |
| 5 to 14 days | Free wall rupture | Tamponade, pulseless electrical activity |
| Weeks to months | Ventricular aneurysm, mural thrombus | Persistent ST elevation, embolic stroke |
| Weeks to months | Dressler syndrome | Autoimmune pericarditis with fever |

**Histology of the infarct:** coagulative necrosis by 4 to 24 hours; neutrophils at 1 to 3 days; macrophages at 3 to 7 days (the weakest point, hence mechanical ruptures); granulation tissue at 1 to 3 weeks; dense collagen scar by 2 months.

**Papillary muscles:** the posteromedial muscle is supplied only by the posterior descending artery, so it ruptures most often (inferior MI). The anterolateral muscle has a dual supply.

- Kumar V, Abbas AK, Aster JC. Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease. 10th ed. Elsevier; 2020. Chapter 12.

Nuggets:
- New systolic murmur days after an MI: papillary muscle rupture :: Days after an infarct (classically inferior, because the posteromedial papillary muscle depends on the posterior descending artery alone), sudden pulmonary edema with a new apical holosystolic murmur means acute mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle rupture.

### QUESTION

Exam: Step 1
System: Cardiovascular
Discipline: Physiology
Competency: Causes & mechanisms
Category: Diseases of the myocardium
Topic: Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
Difficulty: 3
Tags: murmur maneuvers, sudden cardiac death, athlete
Free: yes

Stem:
A 17-year-old boy comes to the office for a preparticipation physical examination before joining his school basketball team. Two months ago, he felt light-headed and nearly fainted while running sprints. His paternal uncle died suddenly at the age of 32 years. His pulse is 64/min, and blood pressure is 118/72 mm Hg. Cardiac examination shows a grade 2/6 harsh crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur heard best at the left lower sternal border; the murmur does not radiate to the carotid arteries. An S4 is heard. An ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy with deep, narrow Q waves in the lateral leads.

Lead-in: Which of the following maneuvers is most likely to increase the intensity of this patient's murmur?

A. Handgrip
B. Passive leg raise
C. Squatting from a standing position
D. Standing up from a squatting position
E. Transient arterial occlusion with bilateral arm cuffs

Answer: D
Key concept: Decreased preload worsens LVOT obstruction in HCM

Explanation:
Exertional presyncope, a family history of sudden death, a harsh systolic murmur at the left lower sternal border that does not radiate to the carotids, an S4, and LVH with deep lateral Q waves point to **hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)** with dynamic left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction.

In obstructive HCM, the hypertrophied septum and the anterior mitral leaflet (pulled forward by systolic anterior motion) narrow the outflow tract. **The smaller the left ventricular cavity, the tighter the obstruction and the louder the murmur.**

**Standing up from a squat** pools blood in the legs and abruptly **decreases venous return (preload)**. The left ventricle fills less, the septum and mitral leaflet come closer together, the outflow gradient rises, and the murmur intensifies. The Valsalva strain phase works the same way.

Rule of thumb: almost every murmur gets louder with more blood in the heart. HCM (and the click of mitral valve prolapse, which moves earlier) are the exceptions: they get louder with **less** volume.

Option explanations:
A. Handgrip increases afterload. Higher aortic pressure keeps the ventricle larger and reduces the outflow gradient, so the HCM murmur softens. Handgrip makes regurgitant murmurs (mitral regurgitation, VSD, aortic regurgitation) louder.
B. Passive leg raise returns blood from the legs, increasing preload and left ventricular size, which softens the HCM murmur.
C. Squatting increases both venous return and afterload, enlarging the ventricle and softening the HCM murmur. Patients with HCM sometimes squat instinctively to relieve symptoms.
D. Correct. Standing from a squat decreases preload, shrinks the left ventricular cavity, and worsens dynamic outflow obstruction.
E. Transient arterial occlusion increases afterload, which softens the HCM murmur while making mitral regurgitation and VSD murmurs louder.

Objective: The murmur of obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy intensifies with maneuvers that shrink the left ventricle (decreased preload such as standing or Valsalva, or decreased afterload) and softens with squatting, leg raise, and handgrip.

Textbook:
**Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)** is the most common inherited cardiomyopathy and a leading cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes.

- **Genetics:** autosomal dominant mutations in sarcomere genes, most often beta-myosin heavy chain (MYH7) and myosin-binding protein C (MYBPC3). Also seen in Friedreich ataxia.
- **Pathology:** asymmetric septal hypertrophy; histology shows disorganized, branching myocytes (myofiber disarray) with interstitial fibrosis.
- **Physiology:** a stiff, hypertrophied ventricle causes diastolic dysfunction (S4). In obstructive HCM, systolic anterior motion of the mitral leaflet produces a dynamic outflow gradient and often mitral regurgitation.
- **Presentation:** exertional dyspnea, chest pain, syncope, or sudden death from ventricular arrhythmia. ECG shows LVH, sometimes deep narrow septal Q waves.

| Maneuver | Effect on LV size | HCM murmur | Most other murmurs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valsalva strain, standing | Smaller (preload falls) | Louder | Softer |
| Squatting, leg raise | Larger (preload rises) | Softer | Louder |
| Handgrip, arm cuffs | Larger (afterload rises) | Softer | Regurgitant murmurs louder |
| Amyl nitrite, vasodilators | Smaller (afterload falls) | Louder | Regurgitant murmurs softer |

- **Management:** beta-blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (verapamil) to slow the heart and improve filling; cardiac myosin inhibitors (mavacamten) for obstructive HCM; implantable cardioverter-defibrillator for high sudden-death risk; septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation for refractory obstruction. Avoid dehydration, diuretics, and vasodilators, which shrink the ventricle.

References:
- Ommen SR, et al. 2024 AHA/ACC Guideline for the Management of Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy. Circulation. 2024;149:e1239-e1311.
- Kumar V, Abbas AK, Aster JC. Robbins & Cotran Pathologic Basis of Disease. 10th ed. Chapter 12.

Nuggets:
- HCM and MVP get louder with less blood in the ventricle :: Valsalva and standing reduce preload and shrink the left ventricle: the HCM murmur intensifies and the mitral valve prolapse click moves earlier. Squatting, leg raise and handgrip do the opposite.

### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1003
Exam: Step 1
System: Cardiovascular
Discipline: Pharmacology
Competency: Causes & mechanisms
Category: Adverse effects of drugs on the heart
Topic: Nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors
Difficulty: 2
Tags: drug interaction, hypotension, cGMP

Stem:
A 58-year-old man is brought to the emergency department 30 minutes after he fainted at home. Earlier this evening he developed substernal chest pressure and took two sublingual tablets of a medication previously prescribed for exertional chest pain. One hour before the chest pain began, he took a medication for erectile dysfunction for the first time. He has hypertension and hyperlipidemia. On arrival, his pulse is 124/min, and blood pressure is 70/40 mm Hg. He is diaphoretic but alert and oriented. An ECG shows sinus tachycardia without ST-segment changes.

Lead-in: Accumulation of which of the following intracellular messengers in vascular smooth muscle best explains this patient's hypotension?

A. Calcium
B. cAMP
C. cGMP
D. Diacylglycerol
E. Inositol trisphosphate

Answer: C
Key concept: cGMP accumulation from nitrate plus PDE-5 inhibitor

Explanation:
This man took **sublingual nitroglycerin** for angina after taking a **PDE-5 inhibitor** (sildenafil, tadalafil, or vardenafil) for erectile dysfunction. Both drugs converge on the same messenger, **cGMP**:

- **Nitrates** are converted to nitric oxide, which activates soluble guanylyl cyclase and **increases cGMP production**.
- **PDE-5 inhibitors** block phosphodiesterase-5, which normally **breaks cGMP down**.

cGMP activates protein kinase G, which activates myosin light-chain phosphatase and lowers cytosolic calcium. Dephosphorylated myosin light chains cannot sustain contraction, so vascular smooth muscle relaxes. With synthesis increased and degradation blocked at the same time, cGMP accumulates massively, causing profound venous and arteriolar dilation, collapse of preload and blood pressure, syncope, and reflex tachycardia.

For this reason nitrates are contraindicated within 24 hours of sildenafil or vardenafil and within 48 hours of tadalafil.

Option explanations:
A. Vascular smooth muscle contraction requires a rise in cytosolic calcium. cGMP-dependent signaling lowers intracellular calcium; calcium accumulation would cause vasoconstriction, not hypotension.
B. cAMP also relaxes vascular smooth muscle (beta-2 agonists, prostacyclin, PDE-3 inhibitors such as milrinone), but nitrates and PDE-5 inhibitors act through cGMP.
C. Correct. Nitrates increase cGMP synthesis and PDE-5 inhibitors prevent its breakdown, so cGMP accumulates and causes severe vasodilation.
D. Diacylglycerol is generated by Gq-coupled receptors (alpha-1 adrenergic, angiotensin II AT1, endothelin) and promotes vasoconstriction through protein kinase C.
E. Inositol trisphosphate, also from the Gq pathway, releases calcium from the sarcoplasmic reticulum and causes contraction, not relaxation.

Objective: Nitrates (nitric oxide donors that increase cGMP synthesis) and PDE-5 inhibitors (which block cGMP breakdown) act synergistically on vascular smooth muscle and together cause life-threatening hypotension.

Textbook:
**Nitrates** (nitroglycerin, isosorbide dinitrate, isosorbide mononitrate) release nitric oxide, which activates soluble guanylyl cyclase, raising cGMP and relaxing vascular smooth muscle.

- **Effects:** venodilation more than arteriolar dilation, which lowers preload and myocardial oxygen demand. At higher doses they also dilate coronary arteries and relieve vasospasm.
- **Uses:** acute angina, acute coronary syndrome, acute decompensated heart failure, hypertensive emergency.
- **Adverse effects:** throbbing headache, flushing, orthostatic hypotension, reflex tachycardia (blunted with beta-blockers), and tolerance with continuous use, so a nitrate-free interval is needed. Industrial nitrate exposure causes "Monday disease" (headache and dizziness on return to work after weekend withdrawal).
- **Avoid in:** recent PDE-5 inhibitor use, right ventricular infarction (preload dependent), severe aortic stenosis, and obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

**PDE-5 inhibitors** (sildenafil, vardenafil, tadalafil) prevent cGMP breakdown in the corpus cavernosum and pulmonary vasculature.

- **Uses:** erectile dysfunction, pulmonary arterial hypertension, benign prostatic hyperplasia (tadalafil).
- **Adverse effects:** headache, flushing, dyspepsia, and blue-tinted vision from cross-inhibition of PDE-6 in the retina (sildenafil).

| Second messenger | Generated by | Effect in vascular smooth muscle |
|---|---|---|
| cGMP | Nitric oxide (nitrates, nitroprusside), natriuretic peptides | Relaxation |
| cAMP | Gs receptors (beta-2, prostacyclin IP), PDE-3 inhibition | Relaxation |
| IP3 and DAG | Gq receptors (alpha-1, AT1, endothelin, vasopressin V1) | Contraction |

References:
- Katzung BG, Vanderah TW. Basic & Clinical Pharmacology. 15th ed. McGraw Hill; 2021. Chapter 12.

Nuggets:
- Never combine nitrates with PDE-5 inhibitors :: Both raise cGMP in vascular smooth muscle (nitrates make more of it; sildenafil and tadalafil stop its breakdown), so together they cause profound hypotension.

Exam: Step 2 CK
System: Cardiovascular
Discipline: Internal Medicine
Competency: Mixed management
Category: Vascular disorders
Topic: Aortic dissection
Difficulty: 3
Tags: chest pain, emergency, hypertension

Stem:
A 61-year-old man is brought to the emergency department 40 minutes after the sudden onset of severe chest pain that radiates to his back. He describes the pain as "tearing." He has a 20-year history of poorly controlled hypertension and has smoked one pack of cigarettes daily for 40 years. His pulse is 108/min, and blood pressure is 182/104 mm Hg in the right arm and 148/90 mm Hg in the left arm. A soft early diastolic decrescendo murmur is heard at the left third intercostal space. An ECG shows left ventricular hypertrophy without ST-segment elevation. CT angiography of the chest shows an intimal flap that begins in the ascending aorta and extends into the proximal descending aorta. Intravenous morphine and an esmolol infusion are started.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

A. Emergency surgical repair
B. Endovascular stent graft of the descending aorta
C. Intravenous heparin infusion
D. Thrombolytic therapy with alteplase
E. Transition to oral metoprolol and outpatient follow-up

Answer: A
Key concept: Stanford type A dissection requires emergency surgery

Explanation:
Tearing chest pain radiating to the back, an inter-arm blood pressure difference greater than 20 mm Hg, a new aortic regurgitation murmur, and an intimal flap on CT angiography confirm **acute aortic dissection**. Because the flap **involves the ascending aorta**, this is a **Stanford type A** dissection.

Type A dissections are surgical emergencies. Untreated, mortality rises by roughly 1% to 2% per hour in the first 48 hours from rupture into the pericardium (tamponade), acute aortic regurgitation, coronary artery occlusion (usually the right coronary artery, causing inferior MI), and branch-vessel malperfusion.

The correct sequence is what this patient is already receiving, then surgery:
1. **Analgesia** (opioids) to blunt the catecholamine surge.
2. **Intravenous beta-blockade first** (esmolol or labetalol) to a heart rate near 60/min, which reduces the force of ventricular ejection (dP/dt) on the aortic wall.
3. Add a vasodilator such as nitroprusside only after beta-blockade if systolic pressure stays above 100 to 120 mm Hg (a vasodilator alone causes reflex tachycardia).
4. **Emergency open surgical repair** for type A.

Option explanations:
A. Correct. Any dissection involving the ascending aorta (type A) requires emergency open surgical repair after initial heart rate and blood pressure control.
B. Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) is used for complicated type B dissections (malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain or hypertension). It does not treat a flap that originates in the ascending aorta.
C. Anticoagulation can extend the dissection and cause catastrophic bleeding. Heparin is contraindicated.
D. Thrombolysis is contraindicated and can be fatal. Dissection can mimic myocardial infarction, which is why it must be excluded before giving fibrinolytics to a patient with chest pain.
E. Medical management alone is appropriate only for uncomplicated type B dissections. A type A dissection discharged on oral therapy carries a very high risk of death.

Objective: Acute aortic dissection involving the ascending aorta (Stanford type A) is treated with emergency surgery after analgesia and intravenous beta-blockade; uncomplicated type B dissections are managed medically.

Textbook:
**Aortic dissection** is a tear in the intima that lets blood split the media, creating a false lumen.

- **Risk factors:** hypertension (most common), Marfan and Ehlers-Danlos syndromes, bicuspid aortic valve, Turner syndrome, cocaine or amphetamine use, pregnancy, aortic instrumentation, blunt trauma. Underlying pathology is often cystic medial degeneration.
- **Presentation:** abrupt tearing or ripping chest pain radiating to the back; pulse or blood pressure differential between arms; new aortic regurgitation murmur; neurologic deficits (carotid involvement); widened mediastinum on chest x-ray.
- **Complications:** pericardial tamponade, acute aortic regurgitation, myocardial infarction (right coronary ostium), stroke, mesenteric or renal ischemia, limb ischemia.

| Classification | Involves | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Stanford A | Ascending aorta (with or without descending) | Emergency surgery |
| Stanford B | Descending aorta only (distal to left subclavian) | Medical therapy; TEVAR if complicated |

- **Diagnosis:** CT angiography if hemodynamically stable; transesophageal echocardiography if unstable or contrast is contraindicated.
- **Medical therapy:** opioid analgesia, intravenous beta-blocker first (esmolol, labetalol) to heart rate about 60/min and systolic pressure 100 to 120 mm Hg, then nitroprusside or nicardipine if needed.

References:
- Isselbacher EM, et al. 2022 ACC/AHA Guideline for the Diagnosis and Management of Aortic Disease. Circulation. 2022;146:e334-e482.

System: Cardiovascular
Discipline: Physiology

Category: Valvular heart disease
Topic: Aortic regurgitation
Difficulty: 5
Tags: pressure-volume loop, Marfan syndrome, valvular disease
Daily: yes

Stem:
A 34-year-old man comes to the office because of progressive shortness of breath on exertion for 6 months and a pounding sensation in his chest when he lies on his left side. He is 196 cm (6 ft 5 in) tall and has long fingers and toes. His pulse is 84/min, and blood pressure is 158/48 mm Hg. His head bobs slightly with each heartbeat. The point of maximal impulse is displaced laterally and inferiorly. A high-pitched, blowing, early diastolic decrescendo murmur is heard along the left sternal border; it is loudest when he sits up, leans forward, and exhales. Echocardiography shows a dilated aortic root and a dilated left ventricle with an ejection fraction of 58%.

Lead-in: Compared with a healthy individual, which of the following sets of changes is most likely present in this patient's left ventricular pressure-volume loop?

A. Decreased end-diastolic volume, decreased stroke volume, preserved isovolumetric phases
B. Increased end-diastolic volume, decreased stroke volume, preserved isovolumetric phases
C. Increased end-diastolic volume, increased stroke volume, no true isovolumetric contraction
D. Increased end-diastolic volume, increased stroke volume, no true isovolumetric relaxation
E. Normal end-diastolic volume, increased peak systolic pressure, preserved isovolumetric phases

Answer: D
Key concept: Aortic regurgitation pressure-volume loop

Concepts:
A = Hypovolemia or small restrictive ventricle
B = Dilated cardiomyopathy (systolic failure)
C = Mitral regurgitation
D = Aortic regurgitation
E = Aortic stenosis

Explanation:
A tall man with long digits and a **dilated aortic root** (Marfan syndrome), a **wide pulse pressure** (158/48 mm Hg), head bobbing with each beat (de Musset sign), a displaced apex, and an **early diastolic decrescendo murmur at the left sternal border** has **chronic aortic regurgitation (AR)**.

Read the loop one feature at a time:

1. **End-diastolic volume increases.** In every diastole the ventricle fills from the left atrium and from the leaking aorta. This chronic volume overload causes eccentric hypertrophy and a large, compliant ventricle.
2. **Total stroke volume increases.** The ventricle ejects its normal forward volume plus the regurgitant volume, producing the high systolic pressure and bounding pulses.
3. **No true isovolumetric relaxation.** Normally, after the aortic valve closes, the ventricle relaxes at a constant volume until the mitral valve opens. In AR the incompetent aortic valve lets blood flow back into the ventricle as soon as ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure, so ventricular volume rises during the period that should be isovolumetric. The left side of the loop slopes to the right instead of falling vertically.

The ejection fraction remains preserved (58%) because the compensated ventricle ejects a large total volume.

Option explanations:
A. A small end-diastolic volume and stroke volume describe reduced preload (hemorrhage, dehydration) or a small, stiff ventricle, not the volume overload of chronic AR.
B. An enlarged ventricle that ejects less blood is the loop of systolic heart failure from reduced contractility, such as dilated cardiomyopathy. This patient's ejection fraction is preserved and his stroke volume is increased.
C. Loss of isovolumetric contraction is the hallmark of mitral regurgitation: as soon as the ventricle begins to contract, blood escapes backward into the left atrium before the aortic valve opens.
D. Correct. Chronic aortic regurgitation increases end-diastolic volume and total stroke volume and eliminates true isovolumetric relaxation.
E. Very high peak systolic pressure with little change in end-diastolic volume is the pressure-overload loop of aortic stenosis, which produces concentric hypertrophy.

Objective: Chronic aortic regurgitation produces a volume-overload pressure-volume loop with increased end-diastolic volume and total stroke volume and no true isovolumetric relaxation, because blood re-enters the ventricle from the aorta as soon as the ventricle relaxes.

Textbook:
**Aortic regurgitation (AR)** is backflow from the aorta into the left ventricle during diastole.

- **Causes:** aortic root dilation (Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos, syphilitic aortitis, ankylosing spondylitis, chronic hypertension, aortic dissection) or leaflet disease (bicuspid aortic valve, infective endocarditis, rheumatic heart disease).
- **Acute AR** (endocarditis, dissection): sudden pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock; the unadapted ventricle cannot accommodate the volume.
- **Chronic AR:** eccentric hypertrophy, large stroke volume, and a wide pulse pressure with high systolic and low diastolic pressure.

**Wide pulse pressure signs:** water-hammer (Corrigan) pulse, head bobbing (de Musset), visible nail-bed capillary pulsations (Quincke), pistol-shot femoral sounds (Traube), femoral to-and-fro bruit (Duroziez), popliteal systolic pressure far above brachial (Hill).

**Murmur:** high-pitched early diastolic decrescendo at the left sternal border, loudest sitting forward in end-expiration and with handgrip. A regurgitant jet striking the anterior mitral leaflet can cause a mid-diastolic rumble (Austin Flint murmur).

| Valve lesion | End-diastolic volume | Stroke volume | Pressure-volume loop hallmark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aortic stenosis | Normal | Decreased | Very high systolic pressure (tall loop) |
| Aortic regurgitation | Increased | Increased (total) | No true isovolumetric relaxation |
| Mitral stenosis | Decreased | Decreased | Small loop; high left atrial pressure |
| Mitral regurgitation | Increased | Increased (total) | No true isovolumetric contraction |

- **Management:** treat hypertension with vasodilators (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers). Valve replacement for symptoms, LVEF 55% or less, or marked ventricular dilation.

References:
- Otto CM, et al. 2020 ACC/AHA Guideline for the Management of Patients With Valvular Heart Disease. Circulation. 2021;143:e72-e227.
- Costanzo LS. Physiology. 7th ed. Elsevier; 2022. Chapter 4.

Topic: Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency
Difficulty: 2
Tags: emphysema, nonsmoker, cirrhosis

Stem:
A 38-year-old woman comes to the office because of progressive shortness of breath on exertion for 2 years. She has never smoked cigarettes and rarely drinks alcohol. Her father died of cirrhosis at the age of 55 years; he did not drink alcohol. Her pulse is 88/min, respirations are 20/min, and blood pressure is 118/76 mm Hg. Breath sounds are decreased bilaterally with a prolonged expiratory phase. Pulmonary function testing shows an FEV1:FVC ratio of 0.58 and a markedly decreased diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide. A CT scan of the chest shows hyperlucency with bullae that are most prominent in the lower lobes.

Lead-in: Which of the following is the most likely mechanism of this patient's lung disease?

A. Chronic mucus hypersecretion from submucosal gland hyperplasia
B. Defective chloride transport across airway epithelium
C. Dynein arm defects causing immotile cilia
D. Smooth muscle hypertrophy with eosinophilic airway inflammation
E. Unopposed elastase activity destroying alveolar walls

Answer: E
Key concept: Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Concepts:
A = Chronic bronchitis
B = Cystic fibrosis
C = Primary ciliary dyskinesia
D = Asthma
E = Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency

Explanation:
A **young nonsmoker** with irreversible airflow obstruction (FEV1:FVC 0.58), a **low DLCO** (loss of alveolar surface), **emphysema concentrated in the lower lobes**, and a father who died of **non-alcoholic cirrhosis** has **alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) deficiency**.

AAT is a serine protease inhibitor made by hepatocytes. Its main job in the lung is to neutralize **neutrophil elastase**. Without it, elastase released by neutrophils in the alveoli digests elastin in the alveolar walls unopposed, destroying entire acini. The result is **panacinar emphysema**, worst in the **lower lobes**, where blood flow and therefore neutrophil traffic are greatest.

The liver disease has a different mechanism: the common severe variant (PiZ) misfolds and polymerizes inside the hepatocyte endoplasmic reticulum, forming PAS-positive, diastase-resistant globules that injure the liver (neonatal hepatitis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma). Smoking dramatically accelerates the lung disease because oxidants inactivate whatever AAT remains.

Option explanations:
A. Submucosal gland hyperplasia (increased Reid index) causes chronic bronchitis, defined by productive cough for 3 months in 2 consecutive years. It occurs in smokers and does not destroy alveoli or produce lower-lobe bullae.
B. Defective CFTR chloride transport is cystic fibrosis: thick secretions, recurrent infections, bronchiectasis (upper lobes), and pancreatic insufficiency, usually beginning in childhood.
C. Dynein arm defects cause primary ciliary dyskinesia (Kartagener syndrome when situs inversus is present), with recurrent sinusitis, bronchiectasis, and infertility.
D. Smooth muscle hypertrophy with eosinophilic inflammation describes asthma, which causes reversible obstruction with a normal or increased DLCO.
E. Correct. Unopposed neutrophil elastase destroys alveolar walls, producing panacinar emphysema of the lower lobes.

Objective: Emphysema in a young nonsmoker, predominantly panacinar and in the lower lobes, especially with family history of liver disease, suggests alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency, in which unopposed neutrophil elastase destroys alveolar walls.

Textbook:
**Alpha-1 antitrypsin (AAT) deficiency** is an inherited protease-antiprotease imbalance.

- **Genetics:** SERPINA1 gene on chromosome 14, codominant alleles. PiMM is normal; PiZZ causes severe deficiency (about 15% of normal levels). PiMZ carriers have intermediate levels and higher risk if they smoke.
- **Lung:** panacinar emphysema with lower-lobe predominance, presenting in the 30s to 40s (earlier in smokers). Obstructive pattern with low DLCO.
- **Liver:** misfolded protein accumulates in the endoplasmic reticulum (PAS-positive, diastase-resistant globules). Causes neonatal cholestatic hepatitis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Liver disease results from retained protein, lung disease from absent protein.
- **Other:** panniculitis, c-ANCA vasculitis.
- **Diagnosis:** low serum AAT level, then phenotyping or genotyping.
- **Treatment:** smoking cessation (the single most important step), intravenous AAT augmentation therapy, standard COPD therapy, lung or liver transplantation.

| Location | Upper lobes | Lower lobes |

Exam: Step 1

Topic: Sarcoidosis
Difficulty: 3
Tags: hypercalcemia, granuloma, vitamin D

Stem:
A 33-year-old woman comes to the office because of fatigue, dry cough, and increased thirst for 3 months. She also had tender red nodules on both shins that resolved without treatment last month. She takes no medications or supplements. Her pulse is 78/min, and blood pressure is 122/78 mm Hg. Examination shows no abnormalities. Serum studies show a calcium concentration of 11.8 mg/dL, phosphorus concentration of 4.1 mg/dL, and parathyroid hormone concentration of 8 pg/mL (N=10-65). A chest x-ray shows bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy. A transbronchial biopsy specimen shows noncaseating granulomas.

Lead-in: Which of the following best explains this patient's hypercalcemia?

A. Autonomous secretion of parathyroid hormone from an adenoma
B. Ectopic secretion of parathyroid hormone-related peptide
C. Inactivating mutation of the calcium-sensing receptor
D. Increased 1-alpha-hydroxylase activity in activated macrophages
E. Increased osteoclastic bone resorption at sites of bone metastasis

Answer: D
Key concept: PTH-independent calcitriol production by granulomas

Concepts:
A = Primary hyperparathyroidism
B = Humoral hypercalcemia of malignancy
C = Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia
D = Granulomatous hypercalcemia
E = Osteolytic metastases

Explanation:
Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, erythema nodosum (the tender shin nodules), and **noncaseating granulomas** establish **sarcoidosis**. Her polydipsia comes from hypercalcemia, which impairs renal concentrating ability (nephrogenic diabetes insipidus).

Normally, the final activation step of vitamin D (25-hydroxyvitamin D to 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, calcitriol) happens in the proximal tubule and is tightly controlled by PTH. In sarcoidosis, **activated macrophages (epithelioid histiocytes) in the granulomas express 1-alpha-hydroxylase that ignores PTH**. Unregulated calcitriol production increases intestinal calcium (and phosphate) absorption, causing hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria. The high calcium appropriately **suppresses PTH**, which is why PTH here is low and phosphorus is normal to high.

Glucocorticoids treat it effectively by suppressing granulomatous inflammation and macrophage 1-alpha-hydroxylase.

Option explanations:
A. Primary hyperparathyroidism produces hypercalcemia with an elevated or inappropriately normal PTH and low phosphorus. This patient's PTH is suppressed.
B. PTH-related peptide (squamous cell carcinomas of lung, head and neck; renal and breast carcinoma) mimics PTH at the kidney, so phosphorus is low and calcitriol is not elevated. PTH is suppressed, but there is no tumor and the biopsy shows granulomas.
C. Inactivating calcium-sensing receptor mutations cause familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia: lifelong mild, asymptomatic hypercalcemia with a normal or slightly high PTH and low urinary calcium.
D. Correct. Granuloma macrophages produce calcitriol independent of PTH, increasing intestinal calcium absorption and suppressing PTH.
E. Osteolytic metastases or multiple myeloma raise calcium through local bone destruction, with suppressed PTH, but there is no malignancy here and calcitriol-mediated absorption explains granulomatous disease.

Objective: Granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis cause PTH-independent hypercalcemia because activated macrophages convert 25-hydroxyvitamin D to calcitriol through unregulated 1-alpha-hydroxylase.

**Sarcoidosis** is a multisystem disease of noncaseating granulomas of unknown cause, most common in women aged 20 to 40 and, in the United States, in Black patients.

- **Pathology:** tight noncaseating granulomas; may contain Schaumann bodies (laminated calcium) and asteroid bodies.
- **Lungs and nodes:** bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, reticular or nodular opacities, restrictive pattern with low DLCO in advanced disease.

Textbook:
**Pulmonary embolism (PE)** most often arises from deep venous thrombosis of the proximal leg veins.

- **Virchow triad:** stasis (immobility, long travel, heart failure), endothelial injury (surgery, trauma), hypercoagulability (malignancy, pregnancy and postpartum, estrogen, inherited thrombophilia).
- **Presentation:** sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia, tachypnea, hemoptysis; massive PE causes hypotension and obstructive shock.
- **Findings:** respiratory alkalosis with hypoxemia and increased A-a gradient; ECG most often sinus tachycardia (S1Q3T3 is specific but uncommon); chest x-ray usually normal (rarely Hampton hump or Westermark sign).

**Diagnostic approach (nonpregnant):**
- Low pretest probability: PERC rule, then D-dimer; a normal D-dimer excludes PE.
- Moderate or high probability: CT pulmonary angiography.
- Contrast allergy or renal failure: V/Q scan.

**Pregnancy:** compression ultrasound if leg symptoms; otherwise chest x-ray, then V/Q scan (normal x-ray) or CT angiography (abnormal x-ray).

**Treatment:**
- Hemodynamically stable: anticoagulation (DOAC or low-molecular-weight heparin).
- Pregnancy: low-molecular-weight heparin. Warfarin is teratogenic and DOACs are avoided.
- Massive PE with hypotension: systemic thrombolysis (or catheter-directed therapy or embolectomy).
- Anticoagulation contraindicated: inferior vena cava filter.

### QUESTION
ID: AQ-1009
Exam: Step 1
System: Renal
Discipline: Pharmacology
Competency: Applying foundational science concepts
Category: Metabolic & regulatory disorders
Topic: Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors
Difficulty: 3
Tags: altitude sickness, acid-base, diuretics

Stem:
A 26-year-old man comes to the clinic 1 week before a trekking trip to a base camp at an elevation of 5300 m (17,400 ft). During a previous trip to high altitude, he had severe headache, nausea, and insomnia. He is otherwise healthy and takes no medications. He is prescribed a drug to begin taking 1 day before his ascent to prevent these symptoms.

Lead-in: Within hours of the first dose, which of the following changes in this patient's urine is most likely?

A. Decreased pH and decreased bicarbonate excretion
B. Decreased pH and unchanged bicarbonate excretion
C. Increased pH and decreased bicarbonate excretion
D. Increased pH and increased bicarbonate excretion
E. Unchanged pH and unchanged bicarbonate excretion

Answer: D
Key concept: Acetazolamide causes bicarbonaturia

Explanation:
The drug used to prevent **acute mountain sickness** is **acetazolamide**, a **carbonic anhydrase inhibitor** that acts in the **proximal convoluted tubule**.

Normally, the proximal tubule reclaims about 80% of filtered bicarbonate. Filtered HCO3- combines with secreted H+ to form H2CO3, which brush-border carbonic anhydrase converts to CO2 and water; CO2 diffuses into the cell, where intracellular carbonic anhydrase regenerates HCO3- for return to the blood.

Blocking carbonic anhydrase stops this cycle, so **bicarbonate stays in the tubular fluid and is excreted**. The urine becomes **alkaline (pH rises)**, and sodium, potassium, and water follow in a mild diuresis.

Why that helps at altitude: hypoxia drives hyperventilation, which causes respiratory alkalosis, and the alkalosis in turn brakes the respiratory drive. Acetazolamide-induced bicarbonate loss creates a **mild hyperchloremic (normal anion gap) metabolic acidosis**, which stimulates ventilation and speeds acclimatization, improving oxygenation, sleep, and headache.

Option explanations:
A. Acidic urine with less bicarbonate is the kidney's response to metabolic acidosis from another cause (such as diarrhea), when the tubules are working normally. Carbonic anhydrase inhibition prevents bicarbonate reclamation.
B. Acetazolamide makes the urine more alkaline, not more acidic.
C. Bicarbonate excretion rises, not falls, when proximal reabsorption is blocked.
D. Correct. Blocking proximal tubule carbonic anhydrase causes bicarbonaturia and an alkaline urine.
E. The urinary changes are rapid and measurable within hours of the first dose.

Objective: Acetazolamide inhibits proximal tubule carbonic anhydrase, causing bicarbonaturia (alkaline urine) and a normal anion gap metabolic acidosis that stimulates ventilation and speeds acclimatization to altitude.

Textbook:
**Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors** (acetazolamide, methazolamide, dorzolamide) block bicarbonate reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule.

- **Uses:** prevention of acute mountain sickness; glaucoma (reduce aqueous humor production); idiopathic intracranial hypertension (reduce CSF production); metabolic alkalosis; urinary alkalinization (uric acid and cystine stones); some epilepsies.
- **Adverse effects:** hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia (more sodium delivered distally exchanges for potassium), paresthesias, calcium phosphate kidney stones (alkaline urine and low citrate), sulfonamide hypersensitivity, and hyperammonemia in cirrhosis (alkaline urine traps less NH4+).
- **Diuresis is self-limited:** as plasma bicarbonate falls, less is filtered, so the drug loses effect within days.

| Variable | Change with acetazolamide |
|---|---|
| Urine pH | Increased |
| Urine bicarbonate | Increased |
| Urine potassium | Increased |
| Serum bicarbonate and pH | Decreased (metabolic acidosis) |
| Serum chloride | Increased (normal anion gap) |
| Serum potassium | Decreased |

- Luks AM, et al. Wilderness Medical Society Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Prevention, Diagnosis, and Treatment of Acute Altitude Illness: 2024 Update. Wilderness Environ Med. 2024;35:2S-19S.
- Katzung BG, Vanderah TW. Basic & Clinical Pharmacology. 15th ed. Chapter 15.

Nuggets:
- Acetazolamide at altitude: alkaline urine, acidic blood :: Blocking proximal tubule carbonic anhydrase dumps bicarbonate into the urine; the resulting metabolic acidosis increases ventilation and counters altitude-induced respiratory alkalosis.

Stem:
A 4-year-old boy is brought to the physician because of a 5-day history of puffiness around his eyes that is worst in the morning, and swelling of his legs. Two weeks ago, he had a mild upper respiratory tract infection. His blood pressure is 96/58 mm Hg. Examination shows periorbital edema, 2+ pitting edema of the lower extremities, and shifting dullness of the abdomen. Urinalysis shows 4+ protein and no blood or casts. Serum studies show an albumin concentration of 1.6 g/dL, total cholesterol concentration of 410 mg/dL, and creatinine concentration of 0.3 mg/dL. Serum complement concentrations are within normal limits.

Lead-in: A kidney biopsy specimen from this patient is most likely to show which of the following on electron microscopy?

A. Diffuse effacement of podocyte foot processes
B. Irregular thickening and splitting of the glomerular basement membrane
C. Mesangial immune complex deposits
D. Subendothelial immune deposits with duplication of the basement membrane
E. Subepithelial immune deposits with spikes of basement membrane

Answer: A
Key concept: Minimal change disease

Concepts:
A = Minimal change disease
B = Alport syndrome
C = IgA nephropathy
D = Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis
E = Membranous nephropathy

Explanation:
This child has **nephrotic syndrome**: heavy proteinuria, hypoalbuminemia (1.6 g/dL), edema, and hyperlipidemia. Normal blood pressure, no hematuria, normal renal function, normal complement, a recent viral infection, and age 4 all point to **minimal change disease (MCD)**, the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children.

The name comes from the biopsy: **light microscopy is normal** and immunofluorescence is negative. The diagnostic lesion is visible only by **electron microscopy: diffuse effacement (flattening) of podocyte foot processes**. Podocyte injury, probably triggered by circulating T-cell-derived factors, destroys the negative charge of the filtration barrier, allowing **selective** loss of albumin.

Because MCD responds to **corticosteroids** in over 90% of children, a typical child is treated with prednisone without a biopsy; biopsy is reserved for atypical features or steroid resistance.

Option explanations:
A. Correct. Minimal change disease shows diffuse podocyte foot process effacement with normal light microscopy and no immune deposits.
B. Irregular thickening, thinning, and splitting of the glomerular basement membrane (basket-weave) is Alport syndrome, a type IV collagen defect with hematuria, sensorineural hearing loss, and eye abnormalities.
C. Mesangial IgA deposits define IgA nephropathy, which classically causes episodic hematuria within days of an upper respiratory infection (a nephritic picture).
D. Subendothelial deposits with basement membrane duplication (tram-track) are seen in membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis and diffuse proliferative lupus nephritis, which cause nephritic features and low complement.
E. Subepithelial deposits with basement membrane spikes are membranous nephropathy, the classic nephrotic syndrome of adults (anti-PLA2R antibodies, hepatitis B, solid tumors, SLE).

Objective: Minimal change disease, the most common cause of nephrotic syndrome in children, has normal light microscopy and diffuse podocyte foot process effacement on electron microscopy, and it responds to corticosteroids.

**Minimal change disease (MCD)**

- **Epidemiology:** most common nephrotic syndrome in children (peak age 2 to 6).
- **Triggers and associations:** recent infections or immunizations, atopy, Hodgkin lymphoma, NSAIDs.
- **Pathology:** normal light microscopy; negative immunofluorescence; podocyte foot process effacement on electron microscopy.
